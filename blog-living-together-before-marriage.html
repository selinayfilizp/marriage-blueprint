Living together before marriage: what the research actually says

For decades the data seemed to say living together first made divorce more likely, which contradicted everyone's common sense. The resolution of that paradox is one of the most useful findings in relationship science, and it is not "cohabitation is bad."

The old headline: the cohabitation effect

From the 1970s through the 1990s, study after study found the same strange result: couples who lived together before marrying divorced at higher rates than couples who did not. It became known as the cohabitation effect, and it was genuinely puzzling. Living together looks like due diligence, a test drive before the purchase. Why would the couples with more information do worse? For years the main explanation was selection: the kind of people who cohabited in eras when it was rare differed in many ways from those who did not. Selection is real, but it turned out not to be the whole story.

The real mechanism: sliding versus deciding

The breakthrough reframe came from Scott Stanley and Galena Rhoades: sliding versus deciding. Many couples do not decide to live together; they slide into it. A lease ends. Rent is absurd. One toothbrush becomes a drawer becomes an address. No conversation ever happens about what the step means.

Then inertia takes over. A shared apartment is genuinely harder to leave than a relationship at separate addresses: the lease, the furniture, the dog, the intertwined routines. Constraints pile up before commitment does. And some couples continue up the escalator, from cohabitation into engagement into marriage, less because they chose each other than because each next step was easier than unwinding.

On this account, cohabitation does not damage relationships. It traps a subset of mediocre ones that would otherwise have ended, and those marriages later show up in the divorce statistics. The problem was never the shared address; it was the unexamined slide.

The evidence that fits the mechanism

Three lines of evidence support the sliding account over the "cohabitation is harmful" account:

  • The commitment-sequencing split. Couples who were clearly mutually committed (engaged, or with explicit marriage plans) before moving in look similar in outcomes to couples who never cohabited. The elevated risk concentrated in couples who moved in before any clear commitment. The address is the same; the deciding is what differs.
  • The randomized trial. In a trial with 662 Army couples, control-group couples who had cohabited before committing divorced at 14.6% over two years versus 7.5% for those who moved in after committing, and PREP-based relationship education essentially erased the difference (7.8% versus 7.4%). You cannot randomize cohabitation, but you can randomize deliberateness, and deliberateness closed the gap. That is strong evidence the active ingredient is the deciding, not the calendar.
  • The generational fade. As cohabitation became the majority path (roughly seven in ten US couples now live together before marrying), the selection effects shrank and analyses of recent cohorts (Manning, Cohen, and others) find the old effect weakened or absent, especially for couples who cohabited with commitment in place.

Honest caveats, in keeping with how we handle evidence: this literature is observational apart from the education trials, selection can never be fully removed, and findings come mostly from US samples. The direction is consistent; the precision of any single number is not the point.

What this means practically

The research does not tell you whether to move in together. It tells you how to do whichever you choose:

  • Have the deciding conversation before the boxes. What does this step mean to each of us? Is it a path toward marriage, a test, or a convenience? Those are three different moves that happen to share a moving van. Misaligned silent answers are the actual risk.
  • Name the exit before you need it. "If this is not working, what happens?" is an unromantic question that removes the inertia trap. Couples who cannot discuss the exit are relying on the lease to hold them together.
  • Do not let constraints outrun commitment. Joint leases, shared pets, merged finances: each is fine after the commitment conversation, and each is a small trap before it.
  • Re-decide at each step. The escalator only takes couples who stop deciding. Moving in, engagement, marriage: each deserves its own on-purpose yes. This is the entire premise of taking a structured assessment before the engagement rather than after: it converts a slide into a decision while the decision is still cheap to make.

If you are already living together and recognize the slide, nothing is lost: the Army trial's lesson is that deliberateness added later still closed the gap. The deciding conversation works retroactively; it is just more awkward, which is a poor reason to skip it.

Frequently asked questions

So does living together before marriage cause divorce or not?

The best-supported reading: no, not by itself. The historical association came substantially from selection and from couples sliding into inertia without commitment. Committed-first cohabiters look like non-cohabiters, and in recent cohorts the overall effect has faded.

Is there a right amount of time to live together before marrying?

The research offers no magic duration, and duration is the wrong variable. Sequencing and deliberateness carry the signal: commitment conversations before constraints.

We slid. Are we doomed?

No. The deciding conversation works whenever you have it, and structured preparation measurably helped exactly this group in the randomized evidence. Awkward now beats expensive later.
